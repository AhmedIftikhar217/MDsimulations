Hidden sources of irreproducibility in simulations of hydrophobic assembly

We ran the same simulation of 100 hexane molecules in water ten times. At 50 ns the runs disagreed about what had formed. By 150 ns they all agreed. The difference was timing, and no single run could have told you so.

Draft manuscript, November 2026. Not yet peer reviewed.

Read the paper (PDF, 5 MB) See what it shows

Background

Oil and water separate because water pays a price to surround a non-polar surface, and burying that surface lowers it. This hydrophobic effect drives protein folding, membrane formation and the self-assembly of the peptides and amphiphiles used to build biomaterials.

All-atom molecular dynamics lets us watch that assembly one molecule at a time. Simulation studies routinely report the cluster size, shape or buried surface area at the end of one trajectory, or a few, tens to hundreds of nanoseconds long, often with error bars from block-averaging that same trajectory. That only works if the trajectory has sampled the states that matter. Assembly from a supersaturated solution is not an equilibrium measurement: nuclei form, grow and merge, and the state at any moment depends on when rare events happen, such as two droplets finding each other.

We tested this in the simplest system that assembles: n-hexane in water. Hexane has no charge, forms no hydrogen bonds and has little conformational freedom, so any irreproducibility cannot be blamed on slow folding or specific interactions. Each run held 100 hexanes among about 24,900 water molecules with 150 mM NaCl at 310 K, in a periodic box 9.2 nm across. Every random input differed between the ten runs: where the molecules started, their initial velocities, and the thermostat and barostat seeds.

What the research shows

At 50 ns, the runs disagreed far more than any one run admitted

Averaged over 45–50 ns, the largest cluster held between 50 and 95 molecules depending on the run, and four of the ten runs still held two droplets. Inside each run, though, nothing seemed to be changing. The error bar a run would report for its largest cluster from block averaging was 0.25 molecules; the actual spread between runs was 17, or 66 times larger. Temperature, used as a control, gave a ratio of 1.4.

A 50-ns study built on one trajectory would have reported a morphology with an error bar about two orders of magnitude too small.

Given time, every run reached the same droplet

Over 145–150 ns all ten runs held a single droplet of 98–100 molecules. Between runs the size of the largest cluster varied by 0.5%, against 21% at 45–50 ns. The disagreement at 50 ns was about timing, not about the outcome.

When the last droplet forms is the unpredictable part

The final droplet was complete anywhere from 33 to 118 ns, with a median of 52 ns. Five runs had reached it by 50 ns and nine by 100 ns. In nine runs the last step was two droplets merging, after they had coexisted for 7–83 ns; in the tenth, one droplet grew by absorbing single molecules and small clusters and never met a second.

Whole droplets diffuse slowly, about 0.06–0.14 nm² per ns, and molecules passed between the last two droplets only about once every 4–7 ns. The last fusion is therefore a rare, random event, and the waiting times fit a shifted exponential: an onset at 33 ns and a mean wait of 30 ns beyond it.

Local structure and energetics reproduced from the start

How molecules packed agreed between runs throughout: the radial distribution function, the 8.3 neighbours in each hexane's first shell, chain conformations (43% of dihedrals gauche inside clusters, 46% in free molecules) and short-time mobility. The early milestones varied little in absolute terms, with a stable nucleus forming at 2.7–8.9 ns in every run. The potential-energy rise on assembly, 940 ± 65 kJ mol−1 across the ten runs, varied between runs by no more than a single run's own error bar.

Two routine setup steps quietly bias the numbers

Concentration. Pressure equilibration shrank the box by 6.8%, so a system built at 200 mM hexane ran at 215 mM. Logging the equilibration shows that half of the contraction happens within 8–9 ps of the barostat switching on. Concentrations should be quoted from the production volume, not the build.

Surface area. The usual way of computing solvent-accessible surface area ignores periodic images, so a droplet that straddles a box face looks partly exposed. One compact droplet of 95 hexanes appeared up to 22 percentage points less buried depending only on where it sat in the box. At 150 ns, when all ten runs held the same droplet, the uncorrected buried share still varied over 12.7 percentage points between runs, against 1.4 with periodic images.

The force field, not the time step, sets the energetics

In this model, assembly raises the potential energy by about 940 kJ mol−1: burying hexane loses more hexane–water attraction than it gains in hexane–hexane and water interactions, so assembly is driven entirely by entropy. Experiments on liquid hydrocarbons imply a slightly favourable enthalpy for this step at 37 °C.

Control simulations place the difference in the force field. Liquid hexane in this model has an enthalpy of vaporisation 10% below experiment (28.3 against 31.6 kJ mol−1), and moving a hexane from its liquid into water is about 7 kJ mol−1 too favourable at 25 °C. Repeating a run at a 2-fs time step changed neither the assembly nor the energy rise.

What to do in your own simulations

These checks cost little. Without them, a trajectory that looks converged can report a state that an independent replica, or the same run continued, would not reproduce. The paper recommends that simulation studies of assembly:

  1. run replicas from independent builds, enough to estimate the spread in the time to the end state, and report the result of each;
  2. compare the spread between runs with the error that each run reports;
  3. follow the number of droplets or aggregates through time, and report a morphology only once it has stopped changing in every replica, or report the time taken to reach it;
  4. give concentrations from the production volume, not the build;
  5. compute surface areas with periodic images and whole molecules;
  6. compare the first production frame with random placement at the same volume;
  7. check the enthalpy of vaporisation and the hydration enthalpy of a single molecule against experiment before interpreting the enthalpy or entropy of assembly;
  8. report droplet fusions and other rare events alongside time-averaged results.

The paper

Hidden sources of irreproducibility in simulations of hydrophobic assembly

Draft manuscript, November 2026. Not yet peer reviewed; the author list is to be confirmed.

Molecular dynamics simulations are routinely used to explain how hydrophobic molecules, peptides and amphiphiles assemble in water, yet whether the assembled state they report is reproducible is rarely tested. Here we use n-hexane, the simplest hydrophobic solute, as a test case. We ran ten fully independent all-atom simulations of 100 hexanes in water at 310 K for 150 ns each. After 50 ns the runs appeared to disagree: the largest cluster contained 50 to 95 molecules, and the spread between runs was 66 times the statistical error that any single run would report. Continuing the runs showed that the disagreement was one of timing, not of outcome. All ten runs ended as a single droplet of 98–100 molecules, varying by 0.5% between runs over 145–150 ns against 21% at 45–50 ns. But the final droplet was complete anywhere between 33 and 118 ns (median 52 ns), and only half the runs had reached it by 50 ns. The time needed to reach the end state, rather than the end state itself, is what a single trajectory cannot predict, and a run can look converged while it waits for the next droplet fusion. Local structure, conformations and the energy change were reproducible throughout, and early assembly milestones varied far less than the time to the final droplet. Two routine setup steps add further bias. Pressure equilibration contracts the box by 6.8%, raising the concentration from a nominal 200 mM to 215 mM. Surface areas computed without periodic images understate burial by up to 22 percentage points, depending on where a droplet sits. In this model, assembly raises the potential energy by about 940 kJ mol−1, because the hexane–water dispersion lost on burial outweighs the hexane–hexane and water interactions gained. Spontaneous assembly is therefore entropy-driven here, whereas experiments imply a slightly favourable enthalpy for this step at 37 °C. Control simulations trace the difference to the force field, not to the 4-fs time step: liquid hexane is less cohesive in this model than in reality, and moving a hexane from its liquid into water is about 7 kJ mol−1 too favourable at 25 °C, whereas runs at 2 fs gave the same assembly and energy change. These results show which quantities a single trajectory can support, and how long it must run before a morphology can be reported, and they motivate a minimal set of checks for simulation studies of assembly.

Methods at a glance

System
100 n-hexanes, about 24,900 OPC water molecules and 150 mM NaCl in a 9.2-nm periodic box (215 mM hexane after equilibration).
Force field
Amber Lipid21 acyl-chain parameters for hexane, the four-site OPC water model and OPC-matched ions.
Simulation
OpenMM 8.4 on NVIDIA A100 GPUs. 4-fs time step with hydrogen-mass repartitioning, Langevin thermostat at 310 K, Monte Carlo barostat at 1 bar, particle-mesh Ewald electrostatics.
Sampling
Ten independent replicas of 150 ns each, 1.5 µs in total, with hexane coordinates saved every 10 ps.
Controls
Two 2-fs runs (with and without mass repartitioning), a 150-ns box of water and ions, liquid hexane at two temperatures, and single hexanes in water for the hydration enthalpy.
Analysis
Clusters from carbon–carbon contacts within 0.5 nm; droplets of at least ten molecules followed by membership; surface areas with periodic images; the spread between runs divided by the error bar of one run, in every 5-ns window.

Simulations ran on the Theia high-performance computing cluster at the University of South Carolina, supported by National Science Foundation Major Research Instrumentation grant 2320292, with help from USC Research Computing.
